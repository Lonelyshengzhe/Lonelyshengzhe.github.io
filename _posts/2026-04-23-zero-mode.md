# Origin of Zero-Energy States in the PXP Model

## 1. Physical setting: the constrained PXP Hilbert space

The PXP model describes a one-dimensional chain of two-level atoms under the Rydberg blockade constraint. Each site can be in a ground state or an excited state, but two neighboring sites cannot both be excited.

We denote a basis configuration by a binary string

$$
|\sigma_1\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_L\rangle,
\qquad 
\sigma_i\in\{0,1\},
$$

where

$$
\sigma_i=1
$$

means that site \(i\) is excited, and

$$
\sigma_i=0
$$

means that site \(i\) is not excited.

The Rydberg blockade constraint is

$$
\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}=0.
$$

Therefore, configurations such as

$$
|0110\rangle
$$

are forbidden because they contain adjacent excitations.

The PXP Hamiltonian can be written schematically as

$$
H_{\mathrm{PXP}}
=
\sum_i P_{i-1}X_iP_{i+1},
$$

where \(X_i\) flips the spin at site \(i\), and \(P_{i-1},P_{i+1}\) project the neighboring sites onto the non-excited state. Thus, a spin can only be flipped if the resulting configuration still satisfies the blockade constraint.

---

## 2. The key structural fact: \(H\) changes excitation parity

Let

$$
N_{\mathrm{ex}}=\sum_i \sigma_i
$$

be the number of excitations in a basis state.

Each term in the PXP Hamiltonian flips exactly one spin. Therefore, it changes the number of excitations by

$$
N_{\mathrm{ex}}\to N_{\mathrm{ex}}+1
$$

or

$$
N_{\mathrm{ex}}\to N_{\mathrm{ex}}-1.
$$

Hence the parity of the excitation number always changes:

$$
(-1)^{N_{\mathrm{ex}}}\to -(-1)^{N_{\mathrm{ex}}}.
$$

Therefore, the Hamiltonian maps even-excitation states to odd-excitation states, and odd-excitation states to even-excitation states:

$$
H:
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{even}}
\longleftrightarrow
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{odd}}.
$$

If we order the constrained Hilbert space basis as

$$
\mathcal H
=
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{even}}
\oplus
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{odd}},
$$

then the Hamiltonian has the block off-diagonal form

$$
H=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B \\
B^\dagger & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here \(B\) is the matrix that connects the odd-excitation sector to the even-excitation sector.

This block off-diagonal structure is the fundamental reason why zero-energy states appear.

---

## 3. Chiral symmetry and spectral reflection

Define the parity or chiral operator

$$
\mathcal C
=
(-1)^{N_{\mathrm{ex}}}.
$$

In the even/odd excitation basis, this operator has the form

$$
\mathcal C
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_{\mathrm{even}} & 0 \\
0 & -I_{\mathrm{odd}}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Because \(H\) always changes excitation parity, we have

$$
\mathcal C H \mathcal C = -H.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\{H,\mathcal C\}=H\mathcal C+\mathcal C H=0.
$$

This is the chiral symmetry, or parity anticommutation relation, of the PXP model.

Now suppose

$$
H|\psi_E\rangle=E|\psi_E\rangle.
$$

Then

$$
H(\mathcal C|\psi_E\rangle)
=
-\mathcal C H|\psi_E\rangle
=
-E(\mathcal C|\psi_E\rangle).
$$

Therefore, if \(E\) is an eigenvalue, then \(-E\) is also an eigenvalue.

Thus, the spectrum is symmetric around zero energy:

$$
E \leftrightarrow -E.
$$

This means that nonzero-energy eigenstates are paired. Zero-energy eigenstates are special because they sit exactly at the center of the spectrum and can remain unpaired.

---

## 4. Linear algebra origin of zero modes

Consider the block form

$$
H=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B \\
B^\dagger & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Let

$$
\dim \mathcal H_{\mathrm{even}}=n_{\mathrm{even}},
\qquad
\dim \mathcal H_{\mathrm{odd}}=n_{\mathrm{odd}}.
$$

A state supported only in the even sector has the form

$$
|\psi\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}
v\\
0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Acting with \(H\),

$$
H|\psi\rangle
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B \\
B^\dagger & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
v\\
0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
B^\dagger v
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore, this state is a zero-energy eigenstate if

$$
B^\dagger v=0.
$$

Thus, even-sector zero modes are elements of

$$
\ker B^\dagger.
$$

Similarly, a state supported only in the odd sector has the form

$$
|\psi\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
w
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and it is a zero-energy eigenstate if

$$
Bw=0.
$$

Thus, odd-sector zero modes are elements of

$$
\ker B.
$$

From the rank-nullity theorem,

$$
\dim\ker B^\dagger
=
n_{\mathrm{even}}-\mathrm{rank}(B^\dagger).
$$

Since

$$
\mathrm{rank}(B^\dagger)\le n_{\mathrm{odd}},
$$

we obtain

$$
\dim\ker B^\dagger
\ge
n_{\mathrm{even}}-n_{\mathrm{odd}}.
$$

Therefore, if

$$
n_{\mathrm{even}}>n_{\mathrm{odd}},
$$

there must be at least

$$
n_{\mathrm{even}}-n_{\mathrm{odd}}
$$

zero-energy states.

Similarly, if

$$
n_{\mathrm{odd}}>n_{\mathrm{even}},
$$

there must be at least

$$
n_{\mathrm{odd}}-n_{\mathrm{even}}
$$

zero-energy states.

Hence the number of zero modes satisfies

$$
Z\ge |n_{\mathrm{even}}-n_{\mathrm{odd}}|.
$$

The quantity

$$
Q=n_{\mathrm{even}}-n_{\mathrm{odd}}
$$

is called the chiral charge or chiral index.

Therefore,

$$
Z\ge |Q|.
$$

This is the basic index argument for the existence of zero-energy states.

---

## 5. Physical picture: an unpaired-state mechanism

The PXP Hamiltonian connects configurations by single spin flips. Since a single spin flip changes excitation parity, the constrained Hilbert space can be viewed as a bipartite graph:

$$
\text{even configurations}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{odd configurations}.
$$

Nonzero-energy states are paired between the two sides of this graph. If one side has more independent configurations than the other, some linear combinations cannot be paired.

These unpaired combinations must remain at zero energy.

Thus, zero modes arise from a destructive-interference mechanism: amplitudes from different even configurations can hop into the same odd configurations and cancel exactly.

---

## 6. Example: \(L=4\) open chain

Consider the open PXP chain with \(L=4\).

The allowed constrained basis states are

$$
|0000\rangle,
|1000\rangle,
|0100\rangle,
|0010\rangle,
|0001\rangle,
|1010\rangle,
|1001\rangle,
|0101\rangle.
$$

The even-excitation states are

$$
|0000\rangle,
\quad
|1010\rangle,
\quad
|1001\rangle,
\quad
|0101\rangle.
$$

The odd-excitation states are

$$
|1000\rangle,
\quad
|0100\rangle,
\quad
|0010\rangle,
\quad
|0001\rangle.
$$

So in the full constrained Hilbert space,

$$
n_{\mathrm{even}}=4,
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{odd}}=4.
$$

The total chiral charge is therefore

$$
Q=4-4=0.
$$

Thus, the full-space index does not guarantee a zero mode.

However, the Hamiltonian also commutes with spatial inversion \(I\). We can therefore decompose the Hilbert space into inversion-even and inversion-odd sectors.

---

## 7. \(I=+1\) sector for \(L=4\)

In the inversion-symmetric sector, a convenient even-excitation basis is

$$
|e_0\rangle=|0000\rangle,
$$

$$
|e_1\rangle=
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\left(
|1010\rangle+|0101\rangle
\right),
$$

$$
|e_2\rangle=|1001\rangle.
$$

A convenient odd-excitation basis is

$$
|o_1\rangle=
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\left(
|1000\rangle+|0001\rangle
\right),
$$

$$
|o_2\rangle=
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\left(
|0100\rangle+|0010\rangle
\right).
$$

Thus,

$$
n_{\mathrm{even},I=+}=3,
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{odd},I=+}=2.
$$

The sector-resolved chiral charge is

$$
Q_{I=+}=3-2=1.
$$

Therefore, there must be at least one zero-energy state in this sector.

Let

$$
|\psi_+\rangle
=
a|e_0\rangle+b|e_1\rangle+c|e_2\rangle.
$$

We require

$$
H|\psi_+\rangle=0.
$$

The Hamiltonian acts as

$$
H|e_0\rangle
=
\sqrt2 |o_1\rangle+\sqrt2 |o_2\rangle,
$$

$$
H|e_1\rangle
=
|o_1\rangle+|o_2\rangle,
$$

$$
H|e_2\rangle
=
\sqrt2 |o_1\rangle.
$$

Therefore,

$$
H|\psi_+\rangle
=
(\sqrt2 a+b+\sqrt2 c)|o_1\rangle
+
(\sqrt2 a+b)|o_2\rangle.
$$

Setting this to zero gives

$$
\sqrt2 a+b+\sqrt2 c=0,
$$

$$
\sqrt2 a+b=0.
$$

Subtracting the second equation from the first,

$$
\sqrt2 c=0,
$$

so

$$
c=0.
$$

Then

$$
b=-\sqrt2 a.
$$

Choosing \(a=-1\), we get

$$
|\psi_+\rangle
=
-|e_0\rangle+\sqrt2 |e_1\rangle.
$$

Substituting back,

$$
|\psi_+\rangle
\propto
-|0000\rangle
+
|1010\rangle
+
|0101\rangle.
$$

This is an explicit zero-energy eigenstate:

$$
H|\psi_+\rangle=0.
$$

The cancellation can be seen directly:

$$
H|0000\rangle
=
|1000\rangle+|0100\rangle+|0010\rangle+|0001\rangle,
$$

$$
H|1010\rangle
=
|1000\rangle+|0010\rangle,
$$

$$
H|0101\rangle
=
|0100\rangle+|0001\rangle.
$$

Therefore,

$$
H\left(
-|0000\rangle+|1010\rangle+|0101\rangle
\right)=0.
$$

All hopping amplitudes into the odd-excitation sector cancel exactly.

## 8. Why the full-space chiral charge is small

For an open chain of length \(L\), let

$$
\Omega_{L,N}
$$

be the number of constrained configurations with \(N\) excitations.

The number of allowed configurations with \(N\) excitations is

$$
\Omega_{L,N}
=
\binom{L-N+1}{N}.
$$

The total chiral charge is

$$
Q_L
=
\sum_{N\ge 0}(-1)^N\Omega_{L,N}
=
\sum_{N\ge 0}
(-1)^N
\binom{L-N+1}{N}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
Q_L
=
\#(\text{even excitation configurations})
-
\#(\text{odd excitation configurations}).
$$

This satisfies the recursion

$$
Q_L=Q_{L-1}-Q_{L-2}.
$$

The reason is:

1. If the last site is \(0\), the contribution is \(Q_{L-1}\).
2. If the last site is \(1\), the previous site must be \(0\), and adding this final \(1\) flips the excitation parity, giving contribution \(-Q_{L-2}\).

With

$$
Q_0=1,
\qquad
Q_1=0,
$$

we obtain

$$
Q_L:
1,0,-1,-1,0,1,1,0,-1,\cdots.
$$

Thus \(Q_L\) is periodic with period \(6\), and

$$
Q_L\in\{0,\pm1\}.
$$

It can be written compactly as

$$
Q_L
=
\frac12
\left[
(-1)^{\lfloor (L+1)/3\rfloor}
+
(-1)^{\lfloor (L+2)/3\rfloor}
\right].
$$

Therefore, the full-space chiral charge gives only a weak lower bound:

$$
Z_L\ge |Q_L|\le 1.
$$

This does not explain the exponentially many zero modes.

---

## 9. Why symmetry resolution gives many zero modes

The important point is that the PXP Hamiltonian also has spatial symmetries.

For open boundary conditions, it commutes with inversion:

$$
[H,I]=0.
$$

For periodic boundary conditions, it also commutes with translation:

$$
[H,T]=0.
$$

Therefore, the Hilbert space can be decomposed into symmetry sectors, for example

$$
I=+1,
\qquad
I=-1,
$$

or, for periodic chains,

$$
k=0,
\qquad
k=\pi,
\qquad
I=\pm1.
$$

Inside each symmetry sector \(\alpha\), the Hamiltonian still has the chiral block form

$$
H_\alpha
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B_\alpha\\
B_\alpha^\dagger & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus each sector has its own chiral charge

$$
Q_\alpha
=
n_{\mathrm{even},\alpha}
-
n_{\mathrm{odd},\alpha}.
$$

The zero-mode bound becomes

$$
Z_\alpha\ge |Q_\alpha|.
$$

The total number of zero modes is therefore bounded by

$$
Z
\ge
\sum_\alpha |Q_\alpha|.
$$

This is stronger than

$$
Z\ge
\left|
\sum_\alpha Q_\alpha
\right|.
$$

The reason is that chiral charges from different symmetry sectors can cancel in the full Hilbert space, but they cannot cancel physically because states in different symmetry sectors do not mix under \(H\).

Thus, symmetry resolution prevents the cancellation of chiral charges.

This is the main mechanism behind the exponentially many zero modes in the PXP model.

---

## 10. Fibonacci growth

The constrained Hilbert space has Fibonacci scaling.

Let \(D_L\) be the number of allowed bitstrings of length \(L\) with no adjacent \(1\)'s.

If the first site is \(0\), the remaining \(L-1\) sites form any allowed string, contributing \(D_{L-1}\).

If the first site is \(1\), the second site must be \(0\), and the remaining \(L-2\) sites form any allowed string, contributing \(D_{L-2}\).

Therefore,

$$
D_L=D_{L-1}+D_{L-2}.
$$

Hence,

$$
D_L\sim \varphi^L,
$$

where

$$
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}
$$

is the golden ratio.

The zero-mode counting in symmetry-resolved sectors inherits this Fibonacci structure.

For example, for open boundary conditions, the lower bounds obtained in Buijsman's paper are

$$
Z_{2l}\ge F_{l+1},
$$

for even length \(L=2l\), and

$$
Z_{2l+1}\ge F_l,
$$

for odd length \(L=2l+1\).

Here \(F_l\) is the Fibonacci number.

Thus the number of zero-energy eigenstates grows exponentially with system size:

$$
Z_L\sim \varphi^{L/2}.
$$

---

## 11. Relation to quantum many-body scars

The zero-energy states discussed here are exact eigenstates at the center of the spectrum.

They are not necessarily the same thing as quantum many-body scar states.

The distinction is:

- Zero modes are protected by chiral symmetry and symmetry-resolved Hilbert-space imbalance.
- Scar states are atypical nonthermal eigenstates that violate the expectations of the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis.
- Some zero modes can be nonthermal and low-entangled, and therefore scar-like.
- But being at zero energy alone does not automatically imply being a scar state.

The PXP model is special because its constrained Hilbert space, chiral symmetry, inversion symmetry, and translation symmetry together produce many exact zero modes, some of which are closely related to the scar physics of the model.

---

## 12. Summary

The origin of zero-energy states in the PXP model can be summarized as follows:

$$
\boxed{
\text{PXP flips one spin}
\Rightarrow
\text{excitation parity changes}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
H=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B\\
B^\dagger & 0
\end{pmatrix}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\{H,\mathcal C\}=0
\Rightarrow
E\leftrightarrow -E
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
Z_\alpha
\ge
|n_{\mathrm{even},\alpha}-n_{\mathrm{odd},\alpha}|
}
$$

The full Hilbert space may have only a small total imbalance,

$$
Q_L\in\{0,\pm1\},
$$

but after resolving inversion and translation symmetries, each symmetry sector has its own imbalance.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
Z
\ge
\sum_\alpha
|Q_\alpha|
}
$$

can be exponentially large.

In physical terms, zero modes are unpaired states in a bipartite constrained Hilbert-space graph. They arise because the PXP Hamiltonian only connects even and odd excitation sectors, and in each symmetry sector one side may have more independent states than the other. The excess states cannot pair with nonzero-energy partners and therefore remain exactly at zero energy.
